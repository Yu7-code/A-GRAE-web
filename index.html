Unveiling Implicit Advantage Symmetry: Why GRPO Struggles with Exploration and Difficulty Adaptation

Zhiqi Yu1,*, Zhangquan Chen2,*, Mengting Liu3, Heye Zhang2, Liangqiong Qu1,†
1The University of Hong Kong  |  2Tsinghua University  |  3Sun Yat-sen University
*Equal Contribution   †Corresponding Author
Huggingface arXiv Code

Abstract

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), particularly GRPO, has become the standard for eliciting LLM reasoning. However, its efficiency in exploration and difficulty adaptation remains an open challenge. In this work, we argue that these bottlenecks stem from an implicit advantage symmetry inherent in Group Relative Advantage Estimation (GRAE). This symmetry induces two critical limitations: (i) at the group level, strict symmetry in weights between correct and incorrect trajectories leaves unsampled action logits unchanged, thereby hindering exploration of novel correct solution. (ii) at the sample level, the algorithm implicitly prioritizes medium-difficulty samples, remaining agnostic to the non-stationary demands of difficulty focus. Through controlled experiments, we reveal that this symmetric property is sub-optimal, yielding two pivotal insights: (i) asymmetrically suppressing the advantages of correct trajectories encourages essential exploration; (ii) learning efficiency is maximized by a curriculum-like transition—prioritizing simpler samples initially before gradually shifting to complex ones. Motivated by these findings, we propose Asymmetric GRAE (A-GRAE), which dynamically modulates exploration incentives and sample-difficulty focus. Experiments across seven benchmarks demonstrate that A-GRAE consistently improves GRPO and its variants across both LLMs and MLLMs.

What is the advantage symmetry in GRPO?

Figure 1. The two-fold implicit advantage symmetry problem of GRAE in GRPO. At the group level, the advantage weights for correct trajectories equal those of incorrect trajectories. This symmetry leads to the logits of low-probability correct paths unchanged within the behavior space, thereby hindering the model's exploration. At the sample level, samples of medium-difficulty exhibit the largest sum of absolute advantage values, which leads to insufficient training on harder data.

Deconstructing the Implicit Advantage Symmetry

Figure 2. Experimental results on breaking group-level symmetry using Qwen2.5-Math-7B. We amplify (Positive-Dominant) or suppress (Negative-Dominant) the advantages of correct trajectories compared to GRPO. Notably, the Negative-Dominant group exhibits a monotonic increase in entropy, suggesting better exploration.

Takeaway 1: At the group level, while suppressing positive advantage incentivizes the model to explore unsampled correct trajectories to improve reasoning ability, it introduces risk of collapse later. A dynamic adjustment mechanism is required.

Figure 3. Experimental results on breaking sample-level symmetry using Qwen2.5-Math-7B. Rescaling advantages to shift focus toward hard or easy queries. Easy-Focused converges rapidly early on, while Hard-Focused excels in later phases.

Takeaway 2: Static difficulty reweighting is insufficient. Optimal sample utility is phase-dependent: easy samples for early learning, transitioning to hard samples as performance plateaus.

Proposed Method: Asymmetric GRAE

We propose the Asymmetric Group Relative Advantage Estimation (A-GRAE) framework. To implement dynamic modulation, we use the batch-wise mean reward as a proxy for training state:

$$ \omega_s = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{B}r_i}{B} $$

where \(B\) denotes the total number of trajectories in the batch and \(\omega_s\) is the mean reward. We introduce a dynamic attention shift at the sample level:

$$ A_i = \frac{\omega_s}{2}\cdot\frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\sqrt{p}} + \frac{1-\omega_s}{2}\cdot\frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\sqrt{1-p}} $$

This mechanism shifts training focus from easy to hard questions as the model’s proficiency improves. At the group level, we propose an attenuation suppression strategy:

$$ A_i^* = \begin{cases} A_i \cdot \min(1, \frac{\omega_s}{\alpha}), & \text{if } A > 0 \\ A_i, & \text{if } A \le 0 \end{cases} $$

This encourages exploration in the early stage while preserving stability later.
